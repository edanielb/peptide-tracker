Peanut Allergy Peptide Shot: What a New Trial Found

If your child has a peanut allergy, you probably read every food label twice and never leave home without an epinephrine pen. A new trial suggests a once-a-month peptide shot might one day take some of that fear away. The Australian biotech Aravax released the results today, November 12, 2026, and the most interesting number in them is a zero.

What Is the PVX108 Peanut Allergy Shot?

PVX108 is an experimental treatment made from peptides. A peptide is a short chain of amino acids, the building blocks of protein. In this case, the peptides are small, carefully chosen fragments of peanut protein.

Here's why that matters. In a peanut allergy, antibodies called IgE latch onto whole peanut proteins and set off the reaction: hives, swelling, vomiting, or worse. The peptide fragments are too small for those antibodies to grab. But they can still be spotted by T cells, the immune cells that run the allergic response behind the scenes.

The idea, known as peptide immunotherapy, is to retrain those T cells to treat peanut as harmless without triggering the reaction itself. PVX108 is given as a small injection into the skin once a month. The FDA has given it Fast Track status, which speeds up review, but it is not approved.

What the Peanut Allergy Trial Found

This was a phase 2 trial, a mid-stage study that tests whether a treatment works and at what dose. It ran at 14 sites in the US and Australia. The main group was 71 children aged 4 to 11, with a smaller safety group of 24 teenagers.

Kids got a monthly shot of a low dose (5 nmol), a higher dose (50 nmol), or a placebo for a year. Then doctors ran a food challenge, carefully feeding each child rising amounts of peanut until a reaction appeared.

On the higher dose, the amount of peanut kids could tolerate rose about 2.4-fold. On placebo it rose 1.2-fold. That gap was statistically significant, meaning it's unlikely to be chance.

Reactions also got milder. Everyone started out reacting at a moderate or severe level. After a year, 67% of reactions in the treated kids were mild or absent, compared with 35% on placebo.

About 39% of the high-dose group could handle at least 443 mg of peanut protein, versus 17% on placebo. One peanut holds roughly 250 to 300 mg.

Of the 71 children, 68 finished the year, and none dropped out because of the treatment.

Now the zero. Nobody had a treatment-related allergic reaction, including anaphylaxis, at either dose. Side effects were mostly mild redness or soreness at the injection spot.

Why It Matters for You

Roughly 1 in 50 US children has a peanut allergy, and most never outgrow it. Today's options work, but they come with trade-offs. Palforzia, the approved oral treatment, means eating small daily doses of peanut protein, and that itself can cause reactions. Xolair, an antibody shot, is given every two to four weeks.

A monthly shot that doesn't set off reactions would be a very different experience for families. That safety record is the part of this trial I find most striking.

Be realistic about the size of the benefit, though. Tolerating one or two peanuts doesn't mean a child can eat a peanut butter sandwich. The goal is protection against an accidental bite, like a cookie someone didn't label.

Two other details are worth noting. Kids on the higher dose said they felt less worried about their allergy. And six months after treatment stopped, the benefits held or even improved. That hints the shot may change the allergy itself, not just mask it.

What to Know Before Trying It

You can't get PVX108 right now. It's only available through clinical trials, and a 71-child study is far too small to settle the question. Larger trials will need to confirm these results.

Side effects tied to the treatment showed up in 13% of kids on the higher dose, versus 4% on placebo. None went beyond grade 2, a moderate level, and most were sore or red skin where the shot went in.

The study only included kids aged 4 to 17. It tells us nothing yet about adults or toddlers.

Please don't try to build tolerance at home by feeding your child small amounts of peanut. Supervised immunotherapy is done in a clinic for a reason.

If this interests you, talk to your child's allergist. Good questions to ask: Is my child a candidate for Palforzia or Xolair? Are there peptide immunotherapy trials near us? Should we redo testing to see where things stand?

Meanwhile, keep doing what works. Read labels, keep two epinephrine auto-injectors on hand, and make sure caregivers and teachers know the plan.

The Bottom Line

In a year-long trial, a monthly peptide shot helped children with peanut allergy tolerate more peanut and have milder reactions, with no treatment-related allergic events. The gains were modest, and the study was small. Still, a treatment that calms the immune system without provoking it would be a real change for families. Full data will be presented at an allergy meeting in Phoenix on November 14, and longer follow-up is expected in late 2027. Until then, your allergist and your epinephrine pen are still your best tools.
