Eloralintide: The Amylin Peptide Boosting Tirzepatide

What if the next leap in weight loss medicine comes from pairing hormones instead of picking one? That's the bet behind eloralintide, an experimental peptide from Eli Lilly. In a 48-week trial of people with type 2 diabetes, adding it to tirzepatide pushed average weight loss to 23.3%, and Lilly brought the results back in front of obesity doctors this weekend at ObesityWeek 2026.

What Is Eloralintide?

Eloralintide is a lab-made peptide, a short chain of amino acids, that copies a natural hormone called amylin. Your pancreas releases amylin after you eat, right alongside insulin. It tells your brain you're full, slows how fast your stomach empties, and helps keep blood sugar from spiking after meals.

Eloralintide is an agonist, which means it switches on the amylin receptor the same way the real hormone does. It's a weekly shot.

Tirzepatide, sold as Mounjaro and Zepbound, works on two other gut hormones, GLP-1 and GIP. Put the two drugs together, a combo Lilly calls EloraTZP, and you're nudging appetite through two separate routes at once. In this trial, people used two pens, one for each drug.

What the Eloralintide Trial Found

The phase 2 study enrolled 367 adults with type 2 diabetes plus overweight or obesity. On average they weighed about 232 pounds and had an A1C of 8.1%. A1C is a measure of your average blood sugar over roughly three months. Under 5.7% is considered normal.

People were split into 10 groups for 48 weeks. Some got eloralintide alone, some got tirzepatide alone, some got both at different doses, and some got a placebo. The lead researcher, Dr. Liana Billings of Endeavor Health in Illinois, first presented the data at the European diabetes meeting (EASD) in Milan in late September.

The top combination, eloralintide 9 mg plus tirzepatide 15 mg, led to 23.3% weight loss. That's about 54 pounds. Tirzepatide alone at its highest dose led to 14.8%, and placebo to 3%.

Look a little closer and it gets more impressive. In that top group, 63% of people lost at least a fifth of their body weight. One in four lost 30% or more.

Blood sugar improved too. A1C fell by up to 2.9 points. In every combination group, at least 90% of people got their A1C under 7%, the usual diabetes target. Up to 77% landed in the normal range.

Billings didn't undersell it. She told Healio, "We have not seen these degrees of weight loss in people with type 2 diabetes."

Why Eloralintide Matters for You

If you live with type 2 diabetes, you probably know weight is harder to lose. The disease itself works against you, and so do insulin and some diabetes pills. People with diabetes usually lose less on these drugs than people without it. So a jump from about 15% to 23% is a big deal for this group.

There's a food-noise angle as well. In exit interviews from a separate eloralintide-only trial, shown as a poster at ObesityWeek, 74% of people said they had fewer food cravings, especially for sweets. About half said they had less food noise, that constant background chatter about eating.

My take? This looks a lot like how blood pressure treatment evolved. One drug helps. Two drugs that work in different ways often help more. Amylin and GLP-1 seem to stack rather than overlap, which Lilly's Rachel Batterham described as an additive effect.

What to Know Before Trying Eloralintide

Eloralintide isn't approved anywhere. You can't fill a prescription for it, and any website selling it as a 'research peptide' is selling an unregulated product of unknown purity. Please skip those.

Side effects were mostly in the gut: nausea, diarrhea, constipation, tiredness and lower appetite. They were more common in the combination groups, mainly early on while doses were climbing. Billings said that was expected when you start two medicines together. Serious side effects showed up in 6.5% of participants. Fewer than 10% in any group stopped because of side effects.

Keep the limits in mind, too. This was a mid-stage trial paid for by Lilly, and the full results aren't in a peer-reviewed journal yet. Forty-eight weeks is less than a year, so we don't know what happens long term or after people stop.

If you have type 2 diabetes and want help with weight, ask your doctor whether you're a candidate for an approved option like tirzepatide or semaglutide today, and whether any clinical trials fit you. If you take insulin or a sulfonylurea, ask how your doses would change. Faster weight loss can push blood sugar too low.

The Bottom Line

Eloralintide adds an amylin signal on top of tirzepatide. In people with type 2 diabetes, the pair produced up to 23.3% weight loss over 48 weeks, well beyond tirzepatide alone, along with big drops in blood sugar. The trade-off was more stomach upset early on. Lilly plans to put both drugs into a single shot for phase 3 trials, so this is still a few years from your pharmacy. For now, it's a promising result worth watching, not something to chase online.
