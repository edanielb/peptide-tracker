Collagen Peptides and Bone Health in Runners: New Study

Do collagen peptides do anything for your bones? Runners pound their skeletons every week, so a small new study asked whether a daily scoop of collagen could help. The early answer is "maybe," with a big asterisk.

What Are Collagen Peptides?

Collagen is the main protein in your skin, tendons, cartilage and bones. Think of it as scaffolding that holds your tissues together. Your body makes it, but production slows with age.

Collagen peptides are collagen cut into short pieces. A peptide is a short chain of amino acids, the building blocks of protein. Short pieces dissolve in water, so the powder stirs into coffee or a smoothie.

Bone is not just calcium. A big share of it is collagen, which gives bone a little flex so it doesn't snap like chalk. That is why researchers keep asking whether eating collagen peptides could help bone. Nobody has a firm answer yet.

Your bones are also never finished. They rebuild constantly, with one set of cells clearing out old bone and another laying down new. Scientists call this bone turnover. Anything that tips the balance toward breakdown, like heavy training on too little fuel, can weaken bone over time.

What the Collagen Peptides Study Found

Researchers at several U.S. universities recruited 22 women aged 18 to 35. All ran at least 35 miles a week. Half took 20 grams of collagen peptides a day for 28 days. The other half took a placebo, a carbohydrate powder with the same calories. Neither the women nor the researchers knew who got what. That setup is called double-blind, and it helps keep bias out.

Blood tests before and after looked at bone markers. These are chemicals that show how fast your body builds or breaks down bone.

In the collagen group, P1NP, a marker of bone building, rose about 5 percent, from 441 to 464 ng/mL. The placebo group didn't change. But when the researchers compared the two groups head to head, the gap fell just short of statistical significance (p = 0.07). That makes it a hint, not proof.

Two other results looked stronger. IL-6, a marker of inflammation, dropped from 5.72 to 4.53 pg/mL in the collagen group while creeping up in the placebo group. And sRANKL, a signal that tells the body to break bone down, fell with collagen and rose with placebo.

Here is the catch. Nobody scanned the runners' bones. The study can't say whether anyone's bones got denser or stronger.

The study also has limits you should know about. Eleven women per group is tiny, and the team didn't calculate in advance how many people they needed. They tested many markers at once, which makes a lucky result easier to stumble on. The authors themselves call for larger trials. That is the right call, and it is why I'd treat these numbers as a reason to keep watching, not a reason to start buying.

Why Collagen Peptides Matter for You

Distance runners are a group worth watching. High mileage plus too little food can thin bones, especially in women, and stress fractures are common. If you run, lift or just want strong bones as you age, you've probably wondered whether a supplement can help.

This study doesn't say it can. It says that over four weeks, some markers moved in a direction researchers like to see. Markers are clues, not outcomes. A blood number going up is not the same as a stronger skeleton.

Still, the inflammation result caught my eye. Hard training causes inflammation, and too much for too long gets in the way of recovery. A small drop is interesting. It needs a bigger trial before anyone leans on it.

For now, the proven bone basics haven't changed: enough calories, enough protein, calcium, vitamin D, and some impact or strength training. Collagen peptides, if you try them, sit on top of that list. They don't replace it.

What to Know Before Trying Collagen Peptides

Collagen peptides are generally well tolerated. Some people report bloating, a heavy stomach or an odd aftertaste. Powders are sold as supplements, so quality varies. Look for a third-party testing seal on the label.

The study used 20 grams a day, which is more than many products suggest. Don't assume more is better, and follow the label.

Check the source if you have food allergies, since collagen often comes from beef, fish or eggshell. Collagen is also low in some essential amino acids, so it shouldn't be your only protein.

Talk to a doctor first if you are pregnant, take medication or have kidney problems. If you've had stress fractures, missed periods or you suspect you're underfueling for your training, see a sports medicine doctor before buying any powder. Those problems matter more than a supplement.

The Bottom Line on Collagen Peptides

Twenty-two runners, four weeks, a few promising blood markers. That is the whole study, and the authors call it a pilot. Collagen peptides may nudge bone chemistry and calm inflammation, but no one has shown they make bones stronger.

If you want to try a scoop anyway, the risk looks low. Keep your expectations modest, keep up the basics, and watch for larger trials that include bone scans.
