Post-Bariatric Hypoglycemia: What a Peptide Trial Found

Imagine losing 100 pounds after gastric bypass, then fainting an hour after lunch. That is life for many people with post-bariatric hypoglycemia, a little-known side effect of weight loss surgery. This weekend, a phase 3 trial of a peptide called avexitide reported that a daily shot cut these blood sugar crashes by more than half.

What Is Post-Bariatric Hypoglycemia?

Hypoglycemia means low blood sugar. Post-bariatric hypoglycemia, or PBH, is low blood sugar that shows up months or even years after weight loss surgery, usually one to three hours after a meal.

Here's why it happens. After gastric bypass, food rushes into the small intestine much faster than before. The gut answers with a huge burst of GLP-1, a hormone that tells the pancreas to release insulin. Too much insulin pulls sugar out of the blood too fast, and it crashes.

Symptoms range from shakiness, sweating and a racing heart to confusion, seizures and passing out. The drug maker, Amylyx, estimates about 8% of people who have had a gastric bypass or sleeve gastrectomy develop it. That's roughly 160,000 people in the U.S. There is currently no FDA-approved treatment.

What the Avexitide Trial Found

The trial, called LUCIDITY, enrolled 78 adults at 21 U.S. sites. All had PBH after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, the most common type of bypass. These were not mild cases. Before the study, 68% had symptoms every day, 47% had passed out at some point, and 28% had been hospitalized.

Participants were randomly assigned to a daily 90 mg avexitide injection or a placebo shot for 16 weeks. Neither they nor their doctors knew who got what.

The main goal was to count Level 2 and Level 3 low blood sugar events. Level 2 means glucose dropped below 54 mg/dL, low enough to be dangerous. Level 3 means a severe episode where the person needed help from someone else.

The results were clear. People on avexitide averaged 0.55 events per week, compared with 1.24 on placebo. That's a 55% reduction. The severe Level 3 events fell by 47%. Readings from continuous glucose monitors, small sensors worn on the skin, backed this up with a 42% drop.

Interestingly, the placebo group improved too, by about 38% from where they started. That happens in a lot of trials. Still, the avexitide group improved by about 73%, and the gap between the two was very unlikely to be chance.

Side effects were mostly mild: diarrhea, plus redness and bruising where the shot went in. No serious side effects were linked to the drug, and body weight didn't change.

Why Post-Bariatric Hypoglycemia Matters for You

Here's the twist I find fascinating. Avexitide works in the opposite direction from Ozempic and Zepbound. Those drugs boost GLP-1 signaling. Avexitide is a GLP-1 receptor antagonist, meaning it sits on the GLP-1 receptor and blocks it, so the pancreas stops overreacting to meals.

It's a good reminder that hormones aren't good or bad. The same signal that helps one person lose weight can knock another person's blood sugar to the floor.

If you've had bariatric surgery, this matters even if you feel fine now. PBH often appears a year or more after the operation, long after people stop checking in with their surgical team. Many people mistake the episodes for anxiety, low energy or a simple bad reaction to sweets.

For the people in this trial, fewer crashes could mean driving again, working without fear, or eating lunch without bracing for what comes next. That's not a small thing.

What to Know Before Trying It

You can't get avexitide yet. Amylyx plans to file for FDA approval by the end of 2026 and hopes to launch in 2027 if it's approved. An expanded access program is running for some patients who qualify.

These numbers also come from the company's own announcement and its presentation at ObesityWeek 2026. A full peer-reviewed paper isn't out yet. The trial was small and lasted 16 weeks, though a 32-week open-label extension is still underway.

If you've had weight loss surgery and get shaky, sweaty or foggy a couple of hours after eating, tell your doctor. Ask whether you should wear a continuous glucose monitor for a while. Ask about a referral to an endocrinologist, a hormone specialist, and about a dietitian. Today's main tools are diet changes, like smaller meals with protein and fewer fast-absorbing carbs, plus some off-label medicines.

One more caution. If you've ever passed out from low blood sugar, talk with your doctor about driving safety before you get behind the wheel.

The Bottom Line

Post-bariatric hypoglycemia is real, often missed, and has no approved treatment. In the LUCIDITY trial, a daily avexitide shot cut low blood sugar events by 55% compared with placebo, with mostly mild side effects. The data still needs peer review and a longer track record. But for the thousands of people who traded obesity for unpredictable crashes, a targeted peptide could finally offer a real option. If this sounds like you, start the conversation with your doctor now.
