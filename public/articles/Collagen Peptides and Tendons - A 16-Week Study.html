Collagen Peptides and Tendons: A 16-Week Study

Why do some people snap back from a sprint or a jump while others feel stiff and slow? Part of the answer sits in your tendons, the cords that link muscle to bone. A 2025 trial tested whether collagen peptides could change how stiff and springy those tissues are.

What Are Collagen Peptides?

Collagen is the main protein in your skin, bones, tendons and ligaments. Think of it as the scaffolding that holds you together. Collagen peptides are short pieces of that protein. Makers break collagen down so it dissolves in water and your gut can absorb it.

You will find collagen peptides sold as powders, drinks and capsules. They usually come from cow, fish or chicken sources. Your body does not send them straight to your tendons. It digests them into amino acids and small fragments, then uses them as it sees fit. Whether that helps tissue like tendon is exactly what researchers want to know.

What the Research Found on Collagen Peptides

The study appeared in Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, a well-known sports science journal. It was a randomized controlled trial, the format scientists trust most for testing a supplement. It was also double-blind, so neither the volunteers nor the researchers knew who got the real thing.

Fifty healthy young men took part. Half drank 10 grams of collagen peptides a day. The others drank a placebo, a look-alike drink with no active ingredient. The test ran for 16 weeks.

By the end, the collagen group showed meaningful gains in both muscle stiffness and tendon stiffness. They also improved in explosive strength, the kind of power you use to sprint, jump or throw. The stiffer the muscle-tendon unit got, the better the explosive strength tended to be.

That link makes sense. A stiffer tendon passes force from muscle to bone more quickly, a bit like a tight rope versus a loose one. Still, I would not read this as a magic fix. I could only verify the headline results, not the full set of numbers, so the size of the gains is something to check in the paper itself.

Why Collagen Peptides Matter for You

You do not need to be an athlete to care about tendons. Every step, stair and grocery bag leans on them. Tendons also tend to get less flexible and less resilient with age, and they heal slowly because they get little blood flow.

If you lift weights, run or play a sport with quick bursts, this result is interesting. It hints that a daily collagen supplement might support the tissue that transfers your power. It does not show that it prevents injuries, because that was not what the study measured.

There is also a plain-English caution. The volunteers were young, healthy men. We do not know if women, older adults or people with sore tendons would see the same thing. One trial of 50 people is a promising start, not the final word. Other research on collagen peptides has pointed the same way, but results across studies vary.

Here is a simple way to picture it. Imagine two rubber bands, one worn and loose, one fresh and firm. The firm one snaps back faster when you let go. Tendons work in a similar way when you push off the ground. The trial suggests collagen peptides may nudge tendons toward the firmer end, at least in people who are already active and healthy.

I find that more interesting than the usual skin-and-joints pitch, because it was measured with a real performance test and not just a questionnaire. Still, stiffness is a means, not a goal. What most people want is to move well and stay injury-free, and that is the part we cannot yet promise.

What to Know Before Trying Collagen Peptides

Collagen peptides are generally well tolerated. Some people report a bloated feeling or a lingering taste. Check the label if you have a fish or egg allergy, since many products use those sources.

Supplements are not tested the way medicines are, so quality varies. Look for products that a third party has tested for purity. Be wary of any brand that promises to heal injuries or reverse aging. Studies, including this one, are often funded or supported by the companies that make the products, so read the fine print on who paid.

Talk to your doctor or a physiotherapist first if you are pregnant, take medication, have kidney problems, or have a tendon injury right now. Collagen is a protein, so it adds calories and protein to your day. It does not replace the basics: progressive training, enough rest, and a diet with plenty of protein and vitamin C, which your body needs to build collagen.

The Bottom Line

In a well-designed 16-week trial, 10 grams a day of collagen peptides raised muscle and tendon stiffness and went along with better explosive strength in young men. That is a real signal worth watching. It is not proof that a scoop will make you faster or protect you from injury. If you are curious, treat collagen peptides as a low-risk extra on top of good training, and keep your expectations modest.
