Petrelintide: A New Weight Loss Peptide Tested in Diabetes

What if a weekly shot could help you lose weight and improve your blood sugar, without leaving you queasy? That is the question behind petrelintide, an experimental peptide that posted new trial results on October 7, 2026.

What Is Petrelintide?

Petrelintide is a lab-made version of amylin. Amylin is a hormone your pancreas releases after you eat, right alongside insulin. A hormone is just a chemical messenger that travels through your blood. Amylin's job is to tell your brain you are full.

Petrelintide is also a peptide, a short chain of amino acids, the same building blocks that make up proteins. Natural amylin breaks down quickly, so scientists built a longer-lasting version you inject once a week under the skin. Zealand Pharma makes it, together with Roche.

Most weight loss shots you have heard of copy a different hormone called GLP-1. Petrelintide works through another route. That difference is worth keeping in mind.

Amylin also seems to help the body respond better to leptin, the hormone that signals you have enough stored energy. In plain terms, it may help you feel full sooner and stay satisfied longer. That is the idea, at least. The trial is how we find out whether it holds up in real people.

What the Research Found About Petrelintide

The trial is called ZUPREME-2. It enrolled 220 adults in the US who had overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes. All were already taking metformin, a common diabetes drug, and some also took an SGLT2 inhibitor, another type of diabetes pill. Everyone also got advice on eating less and moving more.

People were randomly assigned to one of three weekly petrelintide doses or to a placebo, a shot with no active drug. Neither patients nor doctors knew who got what.

After 28 weeks, the petrelintide groups lost between 7.4% and 9.2% of their body weight on average. The placebo group lost 2.0%.

Blood sugar moved too. HbA1c, a lab test that reflects your average blood sugar over about three months, fell by up to 0.65 percentage points on petrelintide. In the placebo group it rose by 0.23.

Stomach side effects were the most common problem. They were mostly mild and mostly showed up while doses were being raised. Only 1.9% of people on petrelintide quit because of them, compared with 1.7% on placebo.

The researchers also kept follow-up going until week 38, so we will eventually learn whether the weight stayed off after the main treatment period. That detail matters, because plenty of weight loss results fade once the shots stop or the dose changes.

One caution. These are topline results from a company press release. The full data has not been published, and outside experts have not reviewed it yet.

Why Petrelintide Matters for You

People with type 2 diabetes often find weight loss harder than others do, and some diabetes medicines cause weight gain. A treatment that helps with both is a big deal.

Nausea is also a main reason people stop weight loss shots. If petrelintide really is gentler on the stomach, more people could stay on treatment long enough to benefit.

The weight loss here is smaller than what you may have seen from the strongest drugs. Eli Lilly's retatrutide, for example, has shown much larger numbers. But this trial was short, and people with diabetes usually lose less weight. Comparing across trials is shaky, so I would not read too much into it.

There is a practical angle too. A weight loss of 7% to 9% may sound modest, but doctors often treat a drop of 5% or more as meaningful for health. Even that much can ease strain on your joints and help your blood pressure and blood sugar. Small numbers on a chart can still change how a person feels day to day.

What interests me most is the different mechanism. Zealand and Roche plan to test petrelintide alongside a GLP-1-type drug. If that works, doctors may one day have more ways to fit treatment to the person.

What to Know Before Trying Petrelintide

You cannot get petrelintide yet. It is still experimental. Phase 3 trials, the large final stage before approval, are just starting, with about 7,000 people expected to join. Approval is likely years away.

Be wary of any website selling petrelintide or similar peptides today. Products sold online outside of clinical trials are not checked like approved medicines, and you cannot be sure what is in the vial.

If you have type 2 diabetes or struggle with your weight, ask your doctor about treatments that are approved now. Good questions include: Which options fit my health history? What side effects should I expect? How will we track my blood sugar?

Never change your diabetes medicine on your own. If you hear about a clinical trial near you, your doctor can help you decide whether it makes sense.

The Bottom Line

Petrelintide produced real weight loss and better blood sugar in people with type 2 diabetes, with few dropouts from stomach problems. That is encouraging. It also comes from a press release, not a peer-reviewed paper. The Phase 3 trials are the real test. For now, treat petrelintide as one to watch, not one to buy.
