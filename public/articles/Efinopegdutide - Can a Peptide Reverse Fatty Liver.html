Efinopegdutide: Can a Peptide Reverse Fatty Liver?

About one in three adults has extra fat stored in their liver, and most of them have no idea. A new trial of efinopegdutide, a weekly peptide shot from Merck, suggests that some of the damage this causes can be reversed. The results came out on November 10, 2026, at The Liver Meeting in Denver, and were published the same day in The Lancet.

What Is Efinopegdutide?

Efinopegdutide is an experimental peptide. A peptide is a short chain of amino acids, the building blocks of protein. Your body uses many of them as messengers.

This one copies two of those messengers at once. The first is GLP-1, the gut hormone behind Ozempic and Wegovy, which helps you feel full. The second is glucagon. Glucagon tells the liver to burn its stored fuel, including the fat that builds up inside it.

Drugs that hit two targets like this are called dual agonists. Merck licensed efinopegdutide from the Korean drug maker Hanmi. It's injected under the skin once a week and isn't approved for any use yet.

What the Efinopegdutide Trial Found

The study was a phase 2b trial, a mid-stage test that checks whether a drug works and at what dose. It enrolled 386 adults with MASH. That stands for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis. In plain English, it's fatty liver disease that has turned into inflammation and liver damage.

Every person had a liver biopsy, a tiny tissue sample, to confirm the diagnosis. All of them already had moderate or advanced fibrosis, which is scarring in the liver. The scale runs from F0 to F4, and these people were at F2 or F3. F4 is cirrhosis.

People got one of three doses of efinopegdutide (4, 7 or 10 mg), a placebo, or the approved drug semaglutide for reference. Treatment lasted a full year.

The main goal was MASH resolution, meaning the inflammation cleared without the scarring getting worse. That happened in 56% to 63% of people on efinopegdutide. On placebo, it happened in just 13%.

The second goal was harder: actually reducing scarring by at least one stage. That's the number liver doctors care about most, because scarring is what leads to cirrhosis. It improved in 52% of people on the 7 mg dose, versus 21% on placebo.

One odd detail stood out to me. The highest dose did worse on scarring (34%) than the middle one. That may be noise in a mid-sized trial, but it's worth watching.

Why Efinopegdutide Matters for You

Fatty liver disease is extremely common, and it rarely causes symptoms until the damage is serious. Many people only find out after a routine blood test shows raised liver enzymes. Extra weight, type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure all raise your risk.

Until recently there was no approved drug for MASH at all. That changed with resmetirom (Rezdiffra) in 2024, and then semaglutide was approved for it in 2025. So efinopegdutide isn't filling an empty shelf. It would be competing with real options.

What makes it interesting is the glucagon half. GLP-1 drugs mostly help the liver indirectly, through weight loss. Glucagon works on the liver itself, nudging it to burn fat. That direct action is the reason Merck is betting on this approach.

For you, the bigger lesson is simpler. Liver scarring was once thought to be permanent. This trial adds to growing evidence that, caught early enough, it can partly heal.

What to Know Before Trying Efinopegdutide

You can't get efinopegdutide outside a clinical trial. Be skeptical of any website claiming to sell it. Research peptides bought online are often mislabeled or impure.

The side effects will feel familiar to anyone who has taken a GLP-1 drug. Nausea hit 29% to 44% of people depending on dose, compared with 11% on placebo. Vomiting and diarrhea were also more common. At the highest dose, 11% stopped treatment because of side effects.

If you're worried about your liver, start with your doctor. Ask whether your liver enzymes have ever been high. You can also ask about a FibroScan, a quick ultrasound-style test that checks for fat and stiffness without a needle.

Diet, exercise and cutting back on alcohol still help a fatty liver, and losing even 5% to 10% of your body weight can make a real difference. If you already have diabetes or obesity, ask whether you qualify for a current MASH treatment or a trial.

The Bottom Line

In a year-long trial, efinopegdutide cleared liver inflammation in more than half of people with MASH, and it reduced scarring in many of them. That's a strong result for a mid-stage study. Still, Merck hasn't announced a timeline for approval, and larger trials will need to confirm it. Nausea is a real trade-off too. For now, the most useful thing you can do is find out whether your own liver is healthy. A simple blood test is a good place to start.
