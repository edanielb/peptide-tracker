Oral Alzheimer's Peptide PRI-002: What the Trial Found

Most new Alzheimer's drugs come as IV drips or shots, with regular brain scans to watch for swelling. So what if a treatment came as a drink you take at home once a day? That's the idea behind PRI-002, an oral Alzheimer's peptide from the German company Priavoid. Today it shared its first big mid-stage results. The honest summary is mixed.

What Is PRI-002, the Oral Alzheimer's Peptide?

PRI-002 is a peptide, a short chain of amino acids, built to go after amyloid-beta. That's the sticky protein that builds up in the brains of people with Alzheimer's disease.

Much of the damage seems to come from small clumps of amyloid called oligomers, not the big plaques. PRI-002 is designed to latch onto those clumps and pull them apart into single, harmless pieces. Priavoid calls it a "detangler."

There's a clever twist in how it's built. It uses D-amino acids, the mirror-image versions of the ones your body normally makes. Your digestive enzymes struggle to break those down. That's why this Alzheimer's peptide can be swallowed instead of injected.

What the Alzheimer's Peptide Trial Found

The PRImus-AD trial enrolled 304 people aged 55 to 80 at 38 sites in six European countries. All had mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia caused by Alzheimer's. They took 300 mg of PRI-002, 600 mg, or a placebo every day for at least 48 weeks. Some kept going for up to 96. Because blood levels of the two doses overlapped so much, the company combined them in its analysis.

Here's the part the press release doesn't lead with. The trial missed its main goal. At 48 weeks, scores on the CDR-SB, a scale doctors use to rate memory, judgment and daily function, weren't meaningfully different from placebo.

The more hopeful numbers came later. At 72 weeks, people on the drug declined about 27% more slowly on a thinking test called ADAS-Cog13. Among people who followed the study plan closely, the gap was 36%. That was the only result to clear the usual bar for statistical significance. Daily-living scores leaned the same way at 72 weeks but didn't clear it.

Safety was the bright spot. Brain swelling, a side effect called ARIA-E, showed up in 0 to 2% of people on PRI-002 versus 1% on placebo. Tiny brain bleeds, called ARIA-H, were somewhat more common on the drug: 8 to 12% versus 5%. The most common complaints were headache, nausea and dizziness.

Why It Matters for You

If someone you love has early Alzheimer's, you already know the current options take real effort. The two approved antibody drugs, lecanemab (Leqembi) and donanemab (Kisunla), slow decline by roughly a quarter to a third over 18 months. They also mean infusions or shots, plus repeated MRI scans, because brain swelling can be serious.

A daily oral Alzheimer's peptide with low swelling rates would feel very different. No clinic chair. Fewer scans.

My read? The safety data look good. The effectiveness data are a maybe. A 36% slowing sounds big, but it came from a smaller slice of patients, at a later time point, and only early enrollees were followed that long. Results like that sometimes hold up in bigger trials. Often they don't.

Still, the gap between drug and placebo grew over time instead of fading. Priavoid also says changes in a blood marker of Alzheimer's, p-tau181, fit with how the drug is supposed to work. That's enough to justify a larger test.

What to Know Before Trying an Alzheimer's Peptide

PRI-002 isn't approved anywhere, and you can't get it outside a clinical trial. If you see it sold online as a "research peptide," walk away. You'd have no idea what's in the vial, and Alzheimer's is not something to experiment with at home.

Keep the limits of this news in mind. These are topline results, meaning a first summary released by the company. Outside scientists haven't reviewed them in a journal yet. Full data are due at medical meetings in 2027.

The trial also focused on one group: people in the early stages of Alzheimer's. We don't know if it would help people with moderate or advanced disease, or prevent it in people without symptoms.

If you or a family member are noticing memory changes, start with a proper diagnosis. Ask the doctor about blood or scan tests for amyloid, whether an approved treatment fits, and whether any trials are recruiting nearby. ClinicalTrials.gov lists them.

The Bottom Line

PRI-002 is an Alzheimer's peptide you swallow, designed to break up toxic amyloid clumps. In a 304-person trial it was well tolerated and rarely caused brain swelling. But it missed its main goal at 48 weeks. Some later results hinted at slower memory decline, especially at 72 weeks. That's a reason for a bigger trial, not for celebrating yet. If those signals hold up, a once-a-day option you take at home could change Alzheimer's care. For now, it's worth watching closely.
