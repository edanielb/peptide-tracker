Collagen Peptides and Skin: What a New Study Found

Can a scoop of powder in your morning coffee change how your skin looks? A new study of middle-aged women says collagen peptides might help. The results are promising, but the study has gaps you should know about.

What Are Collagen Peptides?

Collagen is the protein that gives skin its bounce. Think of it as scaffolding under the surface. Your body makes less of it as you get older, and skin starts to feel thinner and drier.

Collagen peptides are collagen broken into tiny pieces so your body can absorb them. A peptide is just a short chain of amino acids, the building blocks of protein. Most products come from cattle, pigs, or fish. You stir the powder into coffee, smoothies, or water.

Your skin is mostly water and protein. Collagen helps hold both in place. When it breaks down faster than your body rebuilds it, lines deepen and skin loses firmness. That is why people look to supplements, creams, and treatments for help.

The product in this study was a bioactive collagen peptide. That means the maker processed it to target specific effects in the body, not only to supply protein. Sales of these supplements keep growing, so it is fair to ask what the evidence says.

What the Research Found About Collagen Peptides

A team in Brazil published the trial in the journal Dermatology and Therapy in January 2026. It enrolled 119 women aged 35 to 55. Most were not regular exercisers. A total of 114 finished the 12 weeks.

The women were split into three groups. One group took nothing. One took 2.5 grams of collagen peptides a day. The third took 10 grams a day.

Both collagen groups saw better skin elasticity and hydration than they had at the start. Only the 10-gram group had fewer wrinkles. Oiliness, body weight, and body composition did not change.

The researchers also tested blood. Two markers rose in both collagen groups compared with the group that took nothing. One was TGF-beta, a signaling protein involved in tissue repair. The other was Klotho, a protein linked to aging. The rise was bigger at the higher dose.

The authors suggest this points to an immune pathway behind the skin changes. They did not test that idea directly, so treat it as a guess for now.

Now the catch. There was no placebo group, so some of the benefit could come from simply expecting to look better. The team did not calculate its needed group size in advance. The trial was short, and it only covered one type of participant. The supplement maker also supplied the product and paid consulting fees to one author. The authors say the company had no role in the study's design or analysis.

Why Collagen Peptides Matter for You

This study adds one data point to a growing pile. Earlier trials have also found modest gains in skin moisture and firmness from oral collagen. Few have shown dramatic change, and this one does not either.

Dose may matter. In this trial, only the larger daily amount cut wrinkle counts. That is a useful clue if you are comparing labels, though one study cannot settle it.

Think about who might care most. If you already eat plenty of protein and use sunscreen, collagen peptides are an optional extra. If your skin feels dry and tight in your forties or fifties, a 12-week trial of your own may be a low-risk experiment. Just keep the cost in mind. A tub can run well over a month of grocery money.

Timing matters too. The changes showed up over about three months. Do not expect a difference in a week.

I find the blood markers the most interesting part, and also the least proven. A rise in a number is not the same as a better outcome for you. Wear sunscreen either way. It still does more for your skin than any powder.

What to Know Before Trying Collagen Peptides

The paper does not report side effects. In other studies, collagen supplements are usually well tolerated. Some people notice bloating or a heavy feeling in the stomach.

Check the source. Many products come from beef or fish. If you have a fish or shellfish allergy, read the label closely.

Supplements are not tested the way drugs are. Quality varies from brand to brand. Look for products that carry third-party testing, such as a seal from an independent lab.

Watch the label for add-ins. Some blends include vitamin C, hyaluronic acid, or herbal extracts. Those may change the dose of collagen you actually get. The trial used plain collagen peptides, so a simple product makes it easier to compare your results with the research.

Talk to your doctor first if you are pregnant or nursing, have kidney problems, or take regular medication. Ask whether a supplement makes sense for you, what dose to use, and how long to try it before deciding if it is worth the cost.

The Bottom Line

Collagen peptides may modestly improve skin hydration and elasticity, and a higher dose may help with wrinkles. This trial had no placebo group and a company-supplied product, so it is a hopeful signal and not proof. If you try them, pick a tested brand, give it about 12 weeks, and check with your doctor.
