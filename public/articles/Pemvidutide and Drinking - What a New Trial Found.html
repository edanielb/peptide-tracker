Pemvidutide and Drinking: What a New Trial Found

What if a weekly shot meant for weight loss also made that third glass of wine less tempting? That's the question behind a new pemvidutide trial, and the early answer looks like yes. The results were presented on November 9, 2026, at The Liver Meeting, a major liver conference held in Denver.

What Is Pemvidutide?

Pemvidutide is an experimental peptide made by the biotech company Altimmune. A peptide is a short chain of amino acids, the building blocks of protein. Your body uses many peptides as chemical messengers.

This one copies two of them. The first is GLP-1, a gut hormone that tells your brain you're full. It's the same pathway behind semaglutide, the drug in Ozempic and Wegovy. The second is glucagon, a hormone that helps your body burn stored energy, including fat inside the liver.

Because it acts on both, scientists call it a dual agonist. That just means it switches on two receptors instead of one. It's given as a weekly injection, and it isn't approved for any use yet.

What the Pemvidutide Research Found

The study is called RECLAIM. It's a phase 2 trial, the mid-stage test that checks whether a drug works before the large final studies. About 100 adults took part. All had moderate to severe alcohol use disorder, the medical term for drinking a person struggles to control. All were also above a healthy weight, with a BMI over 25.

Half got pemvidutide 2.4 mg once a week. The other half got a placebo. Everyone also received the same standard counseling. The trial ran for 24 weeks.

The main measure was heavy drinking days per week. People on pemvidutide cut 4.2 heavy drinking days a week, while the placebo group cut 2.75. The gap showed up as early as week 4.

Some of the other numbers were more striking. By week 24, 42% of people on the drug had no heavy drinking days at all, compared with 17% on placebo. Alcohol-free days reached 39%, against about 21% on placebo.

A blood marker of drinking called PEth backed this up. It fell sharply on the drug and rose slightly on placebo. I find that the most convincing result, because a blood test doesn't depend on anyone's memory of last Saturday.

People on pemvidutide also lost about 8.7% of their body weight, while the placebo group gained a little. Among those who started with a raised risk score for liver scarring, half moved back into the normal range. Only 17% did on placebo.

Why Pemvidutide Matters for You

Alcohol use disorder is common, and treatment is often missing. A few medicines already exist, such as naltrexone, but many people who could use one never get a prescription.

Look closely at the placebo group, too. Those people also cut back a lot. Counseling and simply tracking your drinking help more than you might expect. The drug added to that effect. It didn't replace it.

Doctors who prescribe GLP-1 drugs have heard the same story for years. Patients say they lose interest in alcohol along with late-night snacks. RECLAIM is one of the first controlled trials to test that idea with a GLP-1 and glucagon combination.

The liver angle matters as well. Heavy drinking and extra weight both strain the liver, and they often show up in the same person. A single treatment that eases both would be useful for a lot of people. Still, 100 people over six months is a small study.

What to Know Before Trying Pemvidutide

You can't get pemvidutide yet outside a clinical trial. Be wary of any website selling it. Unapproved research peptides often vary in purity and dose, and nobody checks what's in the vial.

Side effects were mostly in the gut. Nausea affected 44% of people on the drug versus 24% on placebo. Constipation, diarrhea and vomiting were also more common. About one in five people left the study in each group.

If you're worried about your drinking, you don't have to wait for a new drug. Talk to your doctor and ask whether medicines like naltrexone or acamprosate suit you. If you already take a GLP-1 drug and notice you're drinking less, mention it. And if you drink heavily every day, don't stop suddenly without medical help, because alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous.

What's next? Altimmune is due to meet the FDA in December 2026 to plan a phase 3 trial. The FDA has already given the drug Fast Track status, which can speed up its review.

The Bottom Line

In a small but well-designed trial, pemvidutide helped people with alcohol use disorder drink less, and they lost weight too. That's encouraging. These are company-reported results from one phase 2 study, though, and they haven't appeared in a peer-reviewed journal yet. Bigger trials will show whether the effect holds up. Pemvidutide is one to watch, not one to chase. For now, the most useful step is a simple one. If drinking worries you, bring it up with your doctor. Proven help exists today.
